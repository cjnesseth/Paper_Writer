\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\PENDING{Abstract.} Virginia is the only U.S.\ state to enter, exit, and re-enter a
carbon cap-and-trade program (the Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative, RGGI) within a
five-year span. Because the exit was driven by a change in gubernatorial administration
rather than by economic conditions, the timing of treatment is plausibly exogenous. We
use synthetic control and event-study difference-in-differences methods on public data to
estimate the causal effect of Virginia's RGGI participation on retail electricity prices,
wholesale prices, and \co{} emissions, and we test whether the effects of \emph{exiting}
a carbon market are symmetric to the effects of \emph{entering} it. \PENDING{Headline
estimates for the entry (Jan.\ 2021) and exit (Dec.\ 2023) events; the re-entry event
(Jul.\ 2026) is analyzed prospectively.}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} carbon pricing; cap-and-trade; RGGI; synthetic control;
electricity markets; emissions leakage; policy reversal.\\
\noindent\textbf{JEL Codes:} Q48, Q54, Q58, L94, H23.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Cap-and-trade is the dominant market-based instrument for regulating greenhouse-gas
emissions in the United States, yet credible causal evidence on its effects remains
surprisingly thin. The difficulty is one of inference rather than data: carbon markets are
adopted by jurisdictions that differ systematically from non-adopters, and they are rarely
turned off, so the analyst seldom observes a clean counterfactual for what a participating
economy would have looked like absent the policy. Most of what we know about the Regional
Greenhouse Gas Initiative (RGGI), the longest-running U.S.\ carbon market, comes from
comparisons of participating to non-participating states or from decompositions of observed
emissions trends. These designs have taught us a great deal---most notably that much of the
emissions decline in RGGI states is attributable to the shale-gas boom and the Great
Recession rather than to the cap itself \citep{Murray2015_rggi_attribution}---but they
struggle to isolate the policy's own contribution from the many confounders that move with it.

Virginia offers a rare opportunity to make progress. It is the only U.S.\ state to have
\emph{entered}, \emph{exited}, and is scheduled to \emph{re-enter} a carbon cap-and-trade
program within a five-year span. Virginia joined RGGI in January 2021 under the Virginia
Clean Economy Act \citep{VCEA2020}; following a change in gubernatorial administration, the
Air Pollution Control Board repealed the enabling regulation and the state left the program
after the December 2023 auction \citep{VAAPCB2023_repeal}; and, after a further change in
administration, Virginia is scheduled to re-enter in July 2026. Crucially, the proximate
cause of the 2023 exit was political---a new governor with the authority to direct the
rulemaking---rather than a response to electricity-market or macroeconomic conditions. This
political origin makes the \emph{timing} of the withdrawal plausibly exogenous to the
electricity-price and emissions outcomes we study, which is precisely the condition that
comparison-of-trends designs typically cannot satisfy.

This paper uses Virginia's reversal to ask four questions. First, what was the causal effect
of the December 2023 exit on retail electricity prices, wholesale prices, and carbon-dioxide
emissions? Second, are the effects of \emph{exiting} a carbon market symmetric to the effects
of \emph{entering} one, or does participation leave a durable imprint---through irreversible
investment, for instance---that a withdrawal does not undo? Third, how much of any emissions
change in Virginia leaks across state lines within the PJM Interconnection, the wholesale
market in which Virginia sits? Fourth, does the scheduled 2026 re-entry reproduce the entry
effects, providing an additional, prospective test?

Answering these questions requires a counterfactual Virginia. We construct one with the
synthetic control method \citep{Abadie2010_synth,Abadie2015_comparative,Abadie2021_using_synth},
building a weighted combination of donor states---both non-RGGI states in PJM and states that
remained in RGGI throughout---that tracks Virginia's pre-treatment price and emissions
trajectories. We complement the synthetic control with an event-study
difference-in-differences specification and with synthetic difference-in-differences
\citep{Arkhangelsky2021_sdid}, and we conduct inference appropriate to a single treated unit
and a small number of clusters \citep{Cattaneo2021_prediction,Cameron2008_bootstrap}. The
empirical content of the paper rests entirely on public data---retail prices from the U.S.\
Energy Information Administration, hourly emissions from the EPA's continuous emissions
monitoring system, wholesale prices from PJM, and allowance prices from RGGI---so that the
analysis is fully reproducible and does not depend on proprietary utility records.

The paper makes four contributions. It provides, to our knowledge, the first causal estimate
of the effect of \emph{removing} a carbon market, complementing a literature focused almost
entirely on adoption. It exploits the entry/exit pairing to test for asymmetry and hysteresis
in carbon-pricing effects---a question with direct bearing on whether temporary participation
yields lasting benefits, and one that the policy-reversal literature has raised in other
settings \citep{Greenstone2002_caa,Walker2013_reallocation} but never tested for a carbon
market. It delivers an event-based estimate of within-RTO leakage under sub-national pricing,
sharpening cross-sectional estimates of RGGI leakage \citep{Fell2018_leakage}. And it develops
a paired entry/exit/re-entry research design on a single treated unit that may be of
independent methodological interest for evaluating reversible policies.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background} describes
RGGI and Virginia's participation history. Section~\ref{sec:lit} situates the paper in the
literature. Section~\ref{sec:theory} presents a simple model of how an allowance price enters
retail and wholesale prices and derives the predictions we test.
Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data and Section~\ref{sec:strategy} the empirical
strategy. Section~\ref{sec:threats} addresses threats to identification.
Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:interpretation} report and interpret the results, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Institutional Background}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{RGGI Overview}\label{sec:rggi-overview}
RGGI is a cooperative cap-and-trade program covering carbon-dioxide emissions from
fossil-fired electric generators in a group of Northeast and Mid-Atlantic states. Launched in
2009, it was the first mandatory market-based greenhouse-gas program in the United States. The
program caps regional power-sector emissions and distributes nearly all allowances through
quarterly auctions; covered generators must surrender one allowance per short ton of \co{}
emitted over each multi-year compliance period. Proceeds accrue to the participating states,
which direct the bulk of revenue to energy-efficiency and clean-energy programs
\citep{AnalysisGroup2023_impacts}. Two price-stability mechanisms bound the allowance market:
a Cost Containment Reserve (CCR) releases additional allowances when prices rise above a
trigger, and an Emissions Containment Reserve (ECR) withholds allowances when prices fall
below a floor; auctions also clear no lower than a minimum reserve price.

The program is periodically recalibrated through formal program reviews. The Third Program
Review, finalized in 2025 and effective in 2027, sharply tightens the trajectory: the regional
cap steps down by roughly ten and a half percent of the 2025 budget per year through 2033 and
by about three percent per year thereafter, the minimum reserve price rises to \$9.00 in 2027
and escalates seven percent annually, the offset provisions are eliminated, and the ECR is
replaced by an expanded two-tier CCR \citep{RGGI_thirdreview}. These changes matter for our
setting because they raise the expected stringency---and therefore the expected allowance
price---of the program Virginia is scheduled to re-enter in 2026, a point we return to when
interpreting the re-entry design.

\subsection{The Dominion Energy Context}\label{sec:dominion}
Virginia's electricity market is dominated by Dominion Energy Virginia, a vertically
integrated, rate-regulated utility. Unlike generators in restructured markets, Dominion
recovers prudently incurred costs through retail rates set by the State Corporation Commission
(SCC), and it recovered its RGGI allowance costs through a dedicated rate-adjustment clause,
``Rider RGGI.'' The SCC established the rider in docket PUR-2020-00169, approving a revenue
requirement on the order of \$168~million for the first rate year, and later addressed its
reinstatement and revision in subsequent dockets as allowance costs grew
\citep{DominionSCC_rider}. Because the rider was a separately stated, cost-pass-through line
item, Virginia's exit removed an identifiable component of residential bills, and re-entry
will restore one---an institutional feature we exploit to separate the mechanical
(rider-driven) retail effect from any equilibrium effect operating through wholesale prices.

Three concurrent developments complicate the Virginia setting and motivate the controls and
robustness checks below. Residential rates were rising over the sample period for reasons
unrelated to RGGI; explosive data-center load growth in Northern Virginia was placing upward
pressure on demand and, potentially, on prices \citep{LBNL_datacenters}; and the 2022
Inflation Reduction Act was changing the economics of clean generation nationwide. All three
move electricity outcomes and overlap in time with Virginia's RGGI reversal, so a credible
design must hold them constant. Throughout, we use only public data---SCC filings for the
rider, and the public sources described in Section~\ref{sec:data} for all outcomes; no
proprietary Dominion records are used.

\section{Literature Review}\label{sec:lit}
This paper draws on, and contributes to, four literatures.

\paragraph{The empirical effects of carbon pricing.} A first strand estimates how carbon
markets affect emissions and prices. For RGGI specifically, \citet{Murray2015_rggi_attribution}
decompose the post-2009 emissions decline and attribute the majority to falling natural-gas
prices and the recession rather than to the cap, a finding that frames the identification
challenge we confront. \citet{Fell2018_leakage} document substantial emissions leakage to
neighboring non-RGGI regions, and \citet{Yan2021_rggi} reaches a related conclusion studying
fossil-fuel consumption. On prices, recent work finds that RGGI adoption raised retail
electricity prices in participating states by roughly ten percent in the short run
\citep{SongHochman2025_retail}. Background on the earlier U.S.\ cap-and-trade experience for
$\mathrm{SO}_2$ and $\mathrm{NO}_x$ \citep{Burtraw2009_so2nox} and on the theory of
sub-national carbon pricing \citep{Shobe2012_federalism} rounds out this strand. What unites
these studies is a focus on \emph{adoption}; none estimates the effect of removing a carbon market.

\paragraph{Cost pass-through and incidence.} A second strand asks how emissions costs are
transmitted into electricity prices. In competitive wholesale markets, pass-through can be
substantial and even exceed one in equilibrium \citep{Fabra2014_passthrough}, and emission
costs are reflected in hourly prices in restructured systems
\citep{Hintermann2016_passthrough}. The incidence of regulation across firms can be large
even when aggregate price effects are modest \citep{Bushnell2013_profiting}, and accounting
for entry, exit, and investment dynamics changes the welfare assessment of market-based
regulation \citep{Fowlie2016_dynamics}. This literature informs our model of how the
allowance price enters Virginia's regulated retail rate and PJM wholesale prices, and it
cautions that the wholesale-price response need not equal the rider-driven retail response.

\paragraph{Policy reversal and asymmetry.} A third, smaller strand studies what happens when
environmental regulation is tightened or relaxed. \citet{Greenstone2002_caa} estimates the
industrial-activity effects of Clean Air Act nonattainment designations, and
\citet{Walker2013_reallocation} traces the labor-market adjustment costs of the resulting
sectoral reallocation; \citet{Coglianese2020_coal} disentangles the roles of fuel prices and
regulation in the decline of U.S.\ coal. These papers establish that environmental regulation
can have persistent, partly irreversible effects on the capital stock and workforce---the
mechanism that would generate asymmetry between entry and exit---but the question has not been
posed for a carbon market, where we can observe both directions of treatment on the same unit.

\paragraph{Methodology.} Finally, the paper builds on the synthetic control method
\citep{Abadie2010_synth,Abadie2015_comparative} and its practical guidance
\citep{Abadie2021_using_synth}, on synthetic difference-in-differences
\citep{Arkhangelsky2021_sdid}, and on modern difference-in-differences with multiple periods
\citep{Callaway2021_did}. For inference we use prediction intervals tailored to synthetic
control \citep{Cattaneo2021_prediction} and wild cluster bootstrap methods for settings with
few clusters \citep{Cameron2008_bootstrap}. Our paired entry/exit design extends these tools
to the evaluation of a reversible policy.

\section{Theoretical Framework}\label{sec:theory}
To organize the empirical analysis we set out a deliberately simple partial-equilibrium
account of how an allowance price enters the prices a Virginia consumer faces. The model is
not meant to be estimated structurally; its purpose is to make explicit why the retail and
wholesale responses to RGGI participation may differ, and why the response to exit need not
mirror the response to entry.

\subsection{A Carbon Adder in a Regulated Market}\label{sec:model}
We model the retail price of electricity as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:retail}
  \pretail \;=\; \cgen \;+\; \ctd \;+\; \pallow \cdot \erate \;+\; \markup,
\end{equation}
where $\cgen$ is generation cost (fuel, O\&M), $\ctd$ is transmission and distribution
cost, $\pallow$ is the allowance price ($\dollarMWh$ once scaled by the emissions rate
$\erate$ in $\tonsMWh$ of the marginal generator), and $\markup$ is the regulated markup.
Removing the carbon adder ($\pallow \to 0$) reduces $\pretail$ mechanically through the
rider; the wholesale-price effect depends on whether RGGI-state generators are marginal in
PJM dispatch.

\subsection{Predictions}\label{sec:predictions}
The model yields five testable predictions, which map directly onto the outcomes and tests of
Sections~\ref{sec:strategy}--\ref{sec:interpretation}.

\begin{prediction}[Retail prices]\label{pr:retail}
Retail prices fall by approximately the rider amount on exit and rise by approximately the
new rider on re-entry.
\end{prediction}
\begin{prediction}[Wholesale LMPs]\label{pr:wholesale}
Ambiguous: Virginia generators offer lower prices, but if inframarginal the LMP may not move.
\end{prediction}
\begin{prediction}[Virginia emissions]\label{pr:va-emissions}
Rise if the adder was constraining dispatch of Virginia fossil generators.
\end{prediction}
\begin{prediction}[System-wide emissions]\label{pr:system-emissions}
Ambiguous: depends on whether Virginia generation displaces cleaner or dirtier out-of-state generation (leakage).
\end{prediction}
\begin{prediction}[Asymmetry/hysteresis]\label{pr:asymmetry}
Entry effects may exceed exit effects if 2021--2023 clean investments are irreversible,
efficiency gains persist, or adjustments have ratchet properties.
\end{prediction}

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}
We assemble a monthly panel from public sources only; Table~\ref{tab:data-sources} lists each
series and its origin. Retail electricity prices by customer class come from EIA Form~861M
(monthly) and Form~861 (annual). Wholesale prices are locational marginal prices (LMPs) from
PJM's Data Miner~2, aggregated from hourly to monthly. Carbon-dioxide emissions and gross load
at the generating-unit level come from the EPA's continuous emissions monitoring system, via
the Clean Air Markets Program Data portal, aggregated to the plant, state, and PJM-zone levels.
Allowance prices and quantities come from RGGI's auction records \citep{RGGI_auctions}. We
control for the principal drivers of electricity prices and emissions: natural-gas prices
(EIA Henry Hub and the regionally relevant Transco Zone~5 hub), heating- and cooling-degree
days (NOAA), and state economic conditions (income and population from BEA and the Census).
The Rider~RGGI cost passed to Virginia customers is taken from public SCC filings
\citep{DominionSCC_rider}.

\subsection{Sample Construction}\label{sec:sample}
The unit of analysis is the state-month for the price and emissions panels, and the PJM-zone
month for the wholesale analysis; the sample window is January~2018 through mid-2026, spanning
the pre-entry baseline, the participation period, and the post-exit period. Virginia is the
treated unit. We use two donor pools. The first comprises non-RGGI states in the PJM
Interconnection---West Virginia, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Kentucky, Indiana, North Carolina, and
Tennessee---which share Virginia's wholesale market but never priced carbon. The second
comprises states that participated in RGGI continuously throughout the sample---Maryland,
Delaware, New Jersey, Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maine, New Hampshire, Vermont, Rhode Island,
and New York. The two pools serve complementary purposes: the non-RGGI PJM states isolate the
effect of carbon pricing within a common wholesale market, while the continuous-RGGI states
absorb shocks common to carbon-pricing jurisdictions. Synthetic control selects, data-driven,
the weighted combination of donors that best reproduces Virginia's pre-treatment path.

\section{Empirical Strategy}\label{sec:strategy}

\subsection{Synthetic Control (Primary)}\label{sec:scm}
Our primary estimator is the synthetic control method. For each event and outcome we construct
a synthetic Virginia as a convex combination of donor states whose weights are chosen to
minimize the discrepancy between Virginia and the synthetic unit over the pre-treatment period,
matching on pre-treatment outcome values and on the covariates listed in
Section~\ref{sec:data}. The treatment effect in each post-treatment month is the gap between
observed Virginia and its synthetic counterpart. We estimate the method separately for the two
realized events: entry, with a pre-treatment window of January~2018 through December~2020 and a
post-treatment window running to the exit; and exit, with a pre-treatment window of
January~2021 through the months preceding the December~2023 withdrawal and a post-treatment
window running through the end of the sample. Inference proceeds by permutation: we reassign
the treatment to each donor in turn, compute the resulting placebo gaps, and assess whether
Virginia's gap is extreme relative to the placebo distribution
\citep{Abadie2010_synth,Abadie2021_using_synth}. We supplement the permutation test with the
conformal prediction intervals of \citet{Cattaneo2021_prediction}.

\subsection{Event-Study Difference-in-Differences}\label{sec:did}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eventstudy}
  Y_{st} \;=\; \alpha_s + \gamma_t
  + \sum_{\tau \neq -1} \beta_\tau \,\mathbf{1}[s=\VA]\,\mathbf{1}[t-t^\ast=\tau]
  + X_{st}'\delta + \varepsilon_{st},
\end{equation}
with state fixed effects $\alpha_s$, month fixed effects $\gamma_t$, time-varying controls
$X_{st}$ (gas prices, HDD, CDD), and dynamic effects $\beta_\tau$ relative to $\tau=-1$.
Standard errors clustered at the state level; wild cluster bootstrap given the small number
of clusters. Estimated separately for the exit ($t^\ast=$ Dec.\ 2023/Jan.\ 2024), entry
($t^\ast=$ Jan.\ 2021), and prospectively re-entry ($t^\ast=$ Jul.\ 2026) events.

\subsection{Synthetic Difference-in-Differences}\label{sec:sdid}
As a bridge between the two approaches we report synthetic difference-in-differences
\citep{Arkhangelsky2021_sdid}, which combines the unit reweighting of synthetic control with
the double-differencing of difference-in-differences and additionally reweights time periods.
It is more robust than synthetic control when pre-treatment fit is imperfect and more robust
than two-way fixed effects when the treated unit is not well represented by a simple average
of controls, so agreement across the three estimators provides reassurance that the results are
not an artifact of any single method.

\subsection{Extensions}\label{sec:extensions}
We pursue four extensions. First, we replace the state panel with a PJM-zone panel to study
wholesale prices, treating the Dominion (DOM) zone as the treated unit and other PJM zones as
controls. Second, we estimate emissions effects both for Virginia generators and for PJM as a
whole; the difference identifies the leakage rate---the share of any Virginia emissions change
offset by changes elsewhere in the interconnection. Third, we formally test for asymmetry by
comparing the paired estimates, $|\effexit| = |\effentry|$; a rejection is evidence of
hysteresis. Fourth, where public microdata permit, we examine heterogeneity in the retail-price
effect across customer classes.

\section{Threats to Identification and Robustness}\label{sec:threats}
The design's credibility rests on the assumption that, absent the policy change, Virginia's
outcomes would have evolved like those of its synthetic counterpart. Several threats could
violate this assumption, and we address each with a corresponding check.
Table~\ref{tab:threats} summarizes them.

The most important threat is \emph{anticipation}. Governor Youngkin signaled his intent to
withdraw from RGGI shortly after taking office in January 2022, well before the formal exit in
December 2023, so forward-looking generators and investors may have adjusted in advance. We
address this by re-estimating with alternative treatment dates---the January~2022 announcement,
the June~2023 Board vote, and the December~2023 final auction---and by inspecting the
pre-treatment gaps for evidence of anticipatory breaks. A second concern is \emph{concurrent
policy shocks}: the Inflation Reduction Act and changes in state renewable-portfolio standards
moved clean-energy investment over the same period. We control for renewable capacity additions
and federal-subsidy proxies and show that the estimates are stable to their inclusion. Third,
\emph{gas-price volatility} drives both prices and the dispatch order; we control for regional
gas-hub prices and report gas-adjusted outcomes. Fourth, \emph{data-center load growth} in
Northern Virginia could confound price effects; we control for large-customer load and use
per-megawatt-hour prices rather than total revenue. Fifth, the within-PJM design raises a
\emph{SUTVA} concern, since Virginia's exit may affect other PJM states; we estimate PJM-wide
wholesale effects and bound the magnitude of spillovers. Finally, because the rider is a
mechanical pass-through, we decompose the retail effect into its rider and non-rider
(equilibrium) components, and we control for fleet composition to ensure that capacity
additions and retirements rather than the policy are not driving the results. Inference
throughout uses permutation tests for the synthetic control and the wild cluster bootstrap for
the difference-in-differences estimates, both appropriate to the small number of treated units
and clusters \citep{Cattaneo2021_prediction,Cameron2008_bootstrap}.
